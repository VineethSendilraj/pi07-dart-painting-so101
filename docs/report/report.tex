\begin{abstract}
\noindent
We fine-tune the $\pi_{0.7}$ vision-language-action model to aim a motorized dart blaster, held by a low-cost SO-101 arm, at any cell of a 5$\times$5 grid given the instruction ``shoot $\langle$cell$\rangle$''. A language model (Claude) sits above the policy: it chooses cells to draw a requested picture, inspects the board after each shot and re-issues shots that miss. The policy is trained on 116 teleoperated demonstrations, 16 minutes of data collected in a single session. Across 13 fine-tuning runs, validation error is lowest after about 33 passes over the data (0.287), and every probed checkpoint aims in the commanded row and column order when only the instruction changes. The system completed its live drawing demonstration. We release the dataset in LeRobot format under CC BY 4.0, together with the code, wiring and fine-tuning logs.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Vision-language-action (VLA) models such as $\pi_{0.7}$~\cite{pi07} can be adapted to a new robot and task with tens of minutes of teleoperated data. A single instruction-following policy does not, however, plan toward a multi-step goal or notice when a step has failed. A common remedy is a hierarchy in which a high-level model chooses the next instruction and a low-level policy executes it~\cite{hirobot}. We built such a system during a weekend hackathon around a task whose outcome is easy to check visually but hard to guarantee: hitting specific cells of a foam grid with darts, in order, to draw a shape.

The follower arm's gripper is replaced by a wire loop around the blaster handle, and an ESP32-driven relay replaces the blaster's trigger switch, so firing is a binary action (Fig.~\ref{fig:rig}); Fig.~\ref{fig:system} shows the control loop. The policy observes two 640$\times$480 images (a rear view of the arm and board, and a view from the blaster) and five joint angles at 30\,Hz. It outputs chunks of 50 future steps, each with five absolute joint targets and a trigger value. At inference we execute 25 steps per chunk, limit each joint to $3^\circ$ per step, and fire once the arm is still and the predicted trigger has exceeded 0.5 for three consecutive steps.

Above the policy, Claude receives the requested picture and photographs from both cameras, and issues one \texttt{shoot(cell)} command at a time. After each shot it receives new photographs, so a dart that missed or fell out is detected and the cell is shot again.

\section{Dataset}

An operator aimed the follower with a leader arm and fired with a physical button. A web application proposed the next cell, board clutter and start pose, and replayed each take frame by frame so that it could be kept or discarded (Fig.~\ref{fig:pipeline}). The 116 kept demonstrations contain 29{,}131 frames at 30\,Hz (16.2 minutes) and cover 23 of the 25 cells, with A5 and E5 absent. Each frame holds the rear and wrist images, five follower joint angles, and an action of five leader joint angles plus a trigger that is 1 for the 10 steps after the button press. The set mixes 23 live and 93 dry shots, three levels of board clutter and 17 start poses, and is released in LeRobot v3.0 format~\cite{lerobot} under CC BY 4.0.

\section{Training and Results}

We ran 13 fine-tuning jobs on Physical Intelligence's hosted service, varying the training schedule, batch size, data mixture and base checkpoint (Table~\ref{tab:runs}). The training schedule mattered most: validation error was lowest after about 33 passes over the data on the standard base checkpoint and about 18 passes on an alternative base, and longer training overfit. A larger batch and oversampling the cells of the PI logo did not help, and training only on the 101 takes recorded with the final crosshair overlay was no better than using all 116. The run with the lowest error on the standard base was used for the live demonstration.

To test whether the policy uses the instruction, we gave each checkpoint the same images and joint state while changing only the cell in the prompt. All 24 probed checkpoints produced aims ordered by column and row, so the policy follows the instruction rather than replaying a fixed motion.

\section{Limitations}

We did not measure a hit rate on the real system; our evidence is validation error, the prompt probe and the live demonstration. Because each demonstration continues for 2\,s after the shot with the trigger at 0, the policy rarely produced a clean trigger pulse offline; the stillness rule in Section~2 compensates. Coverage is uneven (two demonstrations each for row~1, A3 and E3), and the camera and board positions were fixed throughout.
