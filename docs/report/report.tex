\noindent\textbf{\textit{Abstract}---Vision-language-action (VLA) models can learn a new manipulation skill from a small number of demonstrations, but a policy that follows one instruction at a time does not, by itself, plan toward a multi-step goal or notice when a step has failed. We study a simple hierarchy for a long-horizon task whose outcome is visible on a target: drawing a picture on a 5$\times$5 grid by firing foam darts from a motorized blaster held by a low-cost SO-101 arm. A language model (Claude) acts as the orchestrator. It turns a request such as ``draw the PI logo'' into a sequence of target cells, inspects the board after every shot, and re-issues shots that miss. A fine-tuned $\pi_{0.7}$ policy executes each command, mapping ``shoot $\langle$cell$\rangle$'' and two camera views to joint trajectories and a trigger signal at 30\,Hz. The policy was trained on 116 teleoperated demonstrations (16 minutes of data) collected in a single session. In an offline probe, every fine-tuned checkpoint aimed in the commanded row and column order, and the full system completed a live drawing demonstration. The dataset, code and hardware design are available at \url{https://github.com/VineethSendilraj/pi07-dart-painting-so101}.}

\section{Introduction}

Recent VLA models such as $\pi_0$~\cite{pi0} and $\pi_{0.7}$~\cite{pi07} can be adapted to a new robot and task with tens of minutes of teleoperated data. Many useful tasks, however, are not a single command but a sequence whose next step depends on how the previous ones went. A common way to handle this is a hierarchy in which a high-level model chooses the next instruction and a low-level policy carries it out~\cite{saycan,hirobot}.

We built such a system over a weekend hackathon around a deliberately unforgiving task. A dart blaster held by an SO-101 arm must hit specific cells of a grid in order to draw a shape. Each shot either lands in the right cell or it does not, the result is visible to a camera, and darts sometimes bounce off. This makes the outcome easy to check visually but hard to guarantee from the policy alone.

Our contributions are modest and practical: (i) a working two-level system in which Claude plans and verifies shots while a fine-tuned $\pi_{0.7}$ policy aims and fires; (ii) a hardware recipe that turns a commercial motorized blaster into a single digital output; and (iii) an open dataset of 116 demonstrations in LeRobot format~\cite{lerobot}, released with the code and wiring.

\section{Related Work}

\textit{Language models over robot skills.} SayCan~\cite{saycan} grounds a language model's proposals in the value functions of pretrained skills, and Hi Robot~\cite{hirobot} uses a high-level VLM to issue language commands to a $\pi_0$ policy and to incorporate user feedback. We follow the same split but use a general-purpose model without fine-tuning, and close the loop with images of the task outcome rather than skill values.

\textit{VLA policies.} RT-2~\cite{rt2} showed that vision-language pretraining transfers to robot control. $\pi_0$~\cite{pi0} adds a flow-matching action expert, $\pi_{0.5}$~\cite{pi05} co-trains on heterogeneous data for open-world generalization, and $\pi_{0.7}$~\cite{pi07} conditions on richer prompts. We fine-tune $\pi_{0.7}$ on a single narrow skill.

\textit{Low-cost teleoperation.} Leader-follower teleoperation with inexpensive arms was popularized by ALOHA~\cite{aloha}; the SO-101 and the LeRobot library~\cite{lerobot} make the same setup available for a few hundred dollars.

\section{System}

\subsection{Hardware}
The blaster is an off-the-shelf motorized dart blaster mounted on its tripod. We removed the SO-101 follower's gripper and connected the arm to the blaster handle with a loop of wire through holes drilled in both; the slight compliance turned out to help the arm move the handle smoothly. The blaster's fire microswitch is replaced by the changeover contacts of a relay driven by an ESP32, so firing becomes one digital output. A rocker switch in the battery line serves as master power: with it off, the relay still clicks and the trigger label is still recorded, which allowed dry demonstrations with identical motion. Darts carry thumbtack tips so that hits stay in the foam. Two USB cameras record at 640$\times$480 and 30\,Hz (Fig.~\ref{fig:rig}).

\subsection{Policy}
At each request the policy receives both camera images, the five follower joint angles and the prompt \texttt{shoot $\langle$cell$\rangle$}. It returns a chunk of 50 future steps, each containing five absolute joint targets and a trigger value. We execute 25 steps before querying again, and limit every joint to $3^\circ$ per step. A shot is fired only if the arm has stopped moving and the predicted trigger exceeds 0.5 for three consecutive executed steps, or if the remainder of the chunk holds the current aim and contains such a run.

\subsection{Orchestrator}
Claude receives the user's request and two photographs: the rear view, which shows which cells already hold darts, and the wrist view, which shows the cell under the cross. It responds with one tool call per turn, either \texttt{shoot(cell)} or \texttt{finish}. After each shot it receives new photographs, so a dart that missed its cell or fell out of the board is detected and the cell is shot again (Fig.~\ref{fig:system}). The orchestrator is told which cells have no training data and avoids them.

\section{Dataset}

An operator aimed the follower using the leader arm and fired with a physical button wired to the ESP32. A small web application proposed the next cell, board clutter and start pose, drew the cross on the wrist view, and replayed every take frame by frame so that it could be kept or discarded. Table~\ref{tab:data} summarizes the 116 kept demonstrations. The trigger label is 1 for the 10 steps after the button press and 0 otherwise; 23 shots were fired with live darts and 93 were dry.

\section{Training and Evaluation}

We fine-tuned $\pi_{0.7}$ through Physical Intelligence's hosted fine-tuning service, running 13 jobs that varied the number of steps, batch size, data mixture and base checkpoint. Table~\ref{tab:runs} lists the directly comparable runs, which used the standard base checkpoint on the full dataset; all 13 runs appear in Table~\ref{tab:allruns} in the appendix. Validation error was lowest after roughly 30--35 passes over the data and increased with longer training; a larger batch did not help, nor did oversampling the cells of the PI logo. An alternative base checkpoint offered by the service (``cosmic'') peaked earlier, at about 18 passes, and reached similar error. Keeping every demonstration was better than training only on the 101 takes recorded with the final crosshair.

\section{Limitations}

We did not measure a hit rate on the real system; our evidence is offline validation error, the prompt probe and the live demonstration. Every demonstration continues for 2\,s after the shot with the arm on target and the trigger at 0, so most on-target frames are labeled ``do not fire.'' Offline, the policy rarely produced a clean trigger pulse, which is why the fire rule in Section~III-B checks for a sustained trigger only once the arm is still. Coverage is uneven (two demonstrations each for row~1, A3 and E3), and the camera and board positions were fixed throughout, so we do not expect the policy to transfer to a different layout without new data.
